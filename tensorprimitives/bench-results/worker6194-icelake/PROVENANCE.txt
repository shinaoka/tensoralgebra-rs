## Why this run exists, and what it overturned

Job 6760092 on Zen2 measured the thread pool at up to 11.6x and D53 recommended it as
the default. This run was the strengthening evidence asked for before the flip: the
same arms on an Intel hierarchy, plus a 64 MiB point at the corpus size. The reading
rule was fixed in advance -- >=1.10 at 64 MiB/t32 meant "matters everywhere",
1.00-1.10 "small contractions only", <0.97 "the pool costs at corpus size, which would
be a real surprise".

It came in at **0.80**, and worse at smaller sizes. `pool / base`, drift-corrected by
each row's own `t1` (which no switch can touch; it read 0.996-1.007 throughout):

   MiB dtype      t2      t4      t8     t16     t32
     1   f64   1.054   1.006   0.591   0.457   0.527
     1   c32   1.028   0.928   0.546   0.403   0.478
    16   f64   0.994   0.875   0.740   0.505   0.587
    64   f64   0.993   0.932   0.872   0.791   0.802
    64   c32   0.997   0.961   0.893   0.779   0.781

**46 of 60 cells below 0.97, worst 0.403** -- the pool is up to 2.5x SLOWER than
per-call spawn here. All four dtypes agree. Per case at 64 MiB, 210-284 of 588 points
fall below 0.90 (36-48%), worst 0.094, which is systematic by any reading of A39.

Shape of the damage: neutral at t2 (1.01-1.08, where the pool has one worker),
degrading through t4-t8, worst at **t16**, partial recovery at t32. Worst at t16 rather
than t32 is not explained.

## The mechanism is not identified, and the a-priori argument was wrong

Recorded before the run: "the pool has no reason to be topology-dependent -- it touches
where threads come from, not what they read." **That is false.** Reusing threads also
reuses their allocator arenas, so each worker gets the *same buffer address* back on
every call where a freshly spawned thread gets a fresh, well-spread one. A thread pool
is an allocation-locality change as much as a thread-lifetime change.

The extra time the pool costs is neither fixed nor proportional:

   MiB  width  base ms  pool ms  delta ms  delta/base
     1     32    0.609    1.046     0.431        0.71
    16     32    2.304    3.944     1.671        0.73
    64     32    5.355    7.484     1.781        0.33
    64      8    8.293    9.284     0.939        0.11
    64     16    6.061    7.857     1.459        0.24

It grows with width and with size, then plateaus. The implementation's fixed costs --
31 serial `notify_one` wakes, 31 threads contending on one completion mutex -- are of
order 100-200 us, an order of magnitude too small.

Two candidates, neither separable from this data:

1. **Shared-L3 conflict between reused buffers.** 32 long-lived per-thread packed-`A`
   buffers at repeating addresses, all contending in ONE 48 MiB 12-way L3, against
   fresh well-spread allocations. Zen2 spread the same buffers over sixteen private
   16 MiB L3s, which is exactly where the pool won. Scales with width and traffic.
2. **Barrier cost at 32 participants**, or the wake pattern interacting with it.

Both say this is **our implementation, not pooling as a concept.** A cheap test of (1):
pad each worker's buffer by a per-worker offset so the addresses cannot alias.

## What did not change

* The Zen2 result stands and was labelled with its machine throughout. The pool really
  is worth up to 11.6x there.
* **The guard's refutation (D52) stands** -- it lost on Zen2, where the pool won.
* **Another control replicated, and it is the best baseline in the repo.** The base arm
  at 64 MiB reproduces the two committed Ice Lake curves to 0.1-1.5%: `f64`
  t8/t16/t32 = 6.14/9.62/10.55 here against worker6016's 6.17/9.67/10.59 and
  worker6150's 6.13/9.61/10.54, and `c64` 3m t8 = 6.96 against 6.96. Three nodes,
  three sessions. It also cross-checks `phase4g`'s 64 MiB path against `phase4f`'s
  `threads` stage, which had never been compared.

Not comparable to anything on ccqlin038, and the absolute speedups are not comparable
to job 6760092's -- different machine. Every ratio here is within-session.
